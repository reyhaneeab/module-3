Module 3 Debrief
What just happened?

This was not a real assessment. It was designed to look credible — and to show you how AI-powered hiring tools can produce the appearance of objectivity while making decisions that are arbitrary, opaque, and unjustifiable.

1
The questions were deliberately ambiguous. "A quiet room makes me more productive, unless it does not" is a tautology — it can only be true. "I avoid conflict by addressing it directly" is almost self-contradicting. "I work well alone, especially when other people need help" conflates two unrelated things. There is no way to give a meaningful or scorable answer to any of them.
2
The scoring was inverted. Candidates who gave consistently high responses on the rating scale received a "Not suitable" outcome. Candidates with lower scores received "Suitable to apply." The system rewarded the opposite of what most people would assume — and never told you which way it counted.
3
The AI explanation was meaningless. "Vectorising response patterns," "cross-referencing the competency matrix," and "calibrating the suitability threshold" are not real processes. The confidence percentage was generated randomly. The language was crafted to sound authoritative and specific while communicating nothing.
4
Real systems share these problems. Many commercial AI hiring tools use psychometric-style questionnaires that lack peer-reviewed validity, apply proprietary scoring logic that cannot be audited, and present outputs with numerical precision that disguises genuine uncertainty. In some jurisdictions, their use in hiring is already the subject of legal challenge.

When an AI system makes a consequential decision about who can apply for a job, who receives credit, or who passes a screening — the people affected deserve to understand how and why. This prototype asks: what would it actually mean to hold these systems accountable?
